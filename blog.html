Jamhuri High School Are Kenya's 2025 Debate Champions

The 2025 TDC National Finals delivered everything a season finale should — sharp arguments, electric energy, and a champion that embodied exactly what this platform is built for.

August 5, 2025 · 4 min read
Read More →

The Nairobi air was thick with anticipation on the morning of August 2nd as regional champions from all twelve circuits arrived for what would become the most fiercely contested National Debate Finals in TDC's history.


Two days. Twelve schools. One stage.


From the opening rounds on Day 1, the calibre was immediately apparent. Schools had arrived not just prepared but transformed — teams that had spent months debating financial literacy motions, climate action frameworks, and youth governance brought everything to the national stage. The season's theme — anchored in financial literacy, economic inclusion, and Kenya's relationship with savings and insurance — produced motions that forced students to think far beyond textbook answers.


Jamhuri High School moved through the competition with a composure that belied the pressure. Their arguments were structured, their rebuttals surgical. Where other teams were reactive, Jamhuri were deliberate. When the final adjudication panel delivered its verdict, there was a sense that the result had been earned over months, not just two days.


Among the highlights was the presence of KDIC Director of Bank Resolution David Irungu, who addressed assembled students and educators on the relationship between financial literacy and national development. “The most effective way to build a savings culture in Kenya,” he said, “is to start the conversation in school — before habits are formed.” TDC's platform, he noted, does exactly that.


Partners KDIC, KBA, and AKI were all present — a testament to the organisation's growing credibility as a vehicle for youth financial education. The debates throughout the season had engaged over 3,200 students across 12 counties on topics ranging from insurance awareness and mobile banking to gender equity in financial access.


The weekend closed — as TDC tradition dictates — with the African Themed Gala Night. Students, coaches, judges, and partners gathered in a celebration of the season. Trophies, speeches, music, and the kind of pride that only comes from having done something genuinely difficult.


Jamhuri High School walked away as champions. But every student who stood at a podium this season was already a winner. That's the point.

141,000 Likes and Counting: How Mount Kenya Region Went Viral

When TDC posted highlights from the Mount Kenya regional, 141,900 likes and 3,900 comments flooded in from Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda. Nobody expected that.

April 28, 2025 · 3 min read
Read More →

The video was a highlight reel. Two minutes of students standing at podiums, of arguments landing, of adjudicators nodding, of a hall erupting. Standard TDC content. Except this time, it wasn't standard at all.


Within 48 hours of the Mount Kenya regional highlights hitting TikTok, 141,900 people had liked the post. Over 3,900 left comments. The audience wasn't just Kenyan — views came flooding in from Uganda, Rwanda, and across East Africa. Schools that had never heard of TDC were tagging each other. Students were asking their teachers how to join.


What makes young people respond that viscerally to a debate video? The answer, we think, is recognition. Somewhere in the footage from Nyeri — in the clarity of the arguments, in the confidence of the students, in the sheer fact that these were teenagers speaking about the world's real problems with intelligence and fire — viewers recognised something they wanted. Either for themselves, or for a younger sibling, or for their school.


The Mount Kenya regional was a strong tournament by any measure. Schools from Nyeri, Kirinyaga, and Meru counties competed across two full days. Adjudication clinics ran alongside the competitive rounds. The motions were drawn from TDC's SDG framework — a curriculum that refuses to let debate be abstract.


But what the virality revealed is bigger than one tournament. It revealed an appetite. Young people across East Africa are hungry for content that takes them seriously. That asks them to think. That says their voices matter in conversations about the world they're going to inherit.


TDC has always known this. The 141,000 likes just confirmed that the rest of the continent is starting to notice.

Why KDIC Chose Debate as a Vehicle for Financial Literacy

The most powerful financial education does not happen in a classroom. It happens when students are challenged to argue for it — in front of peers, under pressure, with evidence.

August 10, 2025 · 5 min read
Read More →

When Kenya Deposit Insurance Corporation first approached TDC about a partnership, the question wasn't whether it could work. The question was why it hadn't happened sooner.


The logic is almost too clean: TDC runs competitive debates for 14–18 year-olds across 12 Kenyan counties. KDIC needs young Kenyans to understand the financial system — deposits, insurance, savings, economic participation. Debate is the only educational format that requires students to not just receive information but defend it, interrogate it, and argue for it in real time.


The partnership put KDIC-themed motions at the centre of TDC's 2025 regional circuit. From Nairobi to the Coast, from Nakuru to Kisumu, students debated savings culture, insurance awareness, gender equality in financial access, and the role of youth in Kenya's economic future. These weren't abstract policy discussions — they were personal. What does it mean for your family if the bank where your parents save has no deposit protection? What changes in your community if women have equal access to financial services?


At the 2025 National Finals, KDIC Director of Bank Resolution David Irungu addressed the gathered students with a directness that resonated. Financial literacy, he argued, isn't a school subject. It's a survival skill. And the earlier young people develop the habits of saving, of understanding insurance, of participating consciously in the economy, the stronger Kenya's financial future becomes.


TDC and KDIC are operating on the same theory of change: that an informed youth is the most powerful asset a country can develop. Debate is the training ground. Financial literacy is the curriculum. Kenya's next generation is the result.


The partnership will continue into the 2026 season — deeper, wider, and with even more schools brought into the conversation.

From Shy Student to Stage Champion: What Debate Does to a Young Person

Every champion we celebrate started the same way — uncertain, nervous, unsure if their voice mattered. TDC's job is to prove that it does.

June 15, 2025 · 4 min read
Read More →

The first time most students walk into a TDC debate session, they are terrified. This is almost universal. It doesn't matter how academically strong they are, how many books they've read, or how confidently they talk to their friends. Standing up in front of peers and arguing a position — on record, with no script — activates a fear that is prehistoric and completely understandable.


And then something happens.


It doesn't happen all at once. It happens in increments — the first time an argument lands and the room reacts, the first time a rebuttal cuts through cleanly, the first time an adjudicator writes something affirming on the feedback sheet. It happens when a student realises that their thinking has value, that their perspective has weight, that the room is listening.


The Daily Nation once described TDC as “turning debate into a meaningful platform where young people meet to discuss issues that matter to society.” What that description doesn't capture is the internal transformation that precedes any debate that matters. The visible confidence of TDC alumni — the ones who go on to university leadership, law, journalism, policy — was built quietly, one session at a time, in school halls across Kenya.


Debate develops critical thinking. That much is well-documented. But TDC's particular structure — the SDG-aligned motions, the regional-to-national ladder, the adjudication culture that gives specific, actionable feedback — creates something more specific. It creates young people who know how to form an argument, how to hold it under pressure, and how to revise it when confronted with better evidence.


These are not skills for the debate hall. They are skills for life.


Every year, TDC celebrates a national champion. But the real story is the student who was afraid to speak in August and confident enough to lead a classroom discussion by December. That student doesn't get a trophy. But they get something more durable.

Teachers at the NSE: Building Kenya's Next Generation of Debate Coaches

The NSE is usually a place for trading floors. In 2022, it became a classroom for the teachers shaping Kenya's next generation of debaters.

October 20, 2022 · 3 min read
Read More →

The Nairobi Securities Exchange is not a typical venue for a teachers' workshop. That, in a way, was the point.


When TDC partnered with the NSE to host its 2022 professional development workshop, the setting sent a message before a single session had started: debate isn't just a classroom activity. It belongs in the institutions that shape how society works.


Teachers from schools across Nairobi spent the day learning three things: how to structure a school debate programme from scratch, how to adjudicate fairly and give feedback that actually improves student performance, and how to embed financial literacy — specifically, understanding Kenya's capital markets and economic institutions — into the motions their students debate.


The last point matters because it's easy to run a debate club. It's harder to run one that connects students to the real world. TDC's SDG framework gives teachers a ready-made curriculum — one that links formal debate practice to issues like decent work, reduced inequality, and strong institutions. The NSE workshop gave them the tools to use it.


Participants left with a session plan, an adjudication rubric, a set of ready-to-use motions, and — perhaps most importantly — a network of fellow teachers who are doing the same work across different schools.


The vision TDC is working toward is a Kenya where every school that wants to run a credible debate programme can do so — because its teachers have been trained, supported, and connected. Workshops like the NSE session are how that vision becomes real.

Debate and the SDGs: Why Kenya's Youth Should Argue About the World's Biggest Problems

The 17 SDGs are not just policy targets for governments. At TDC, they are the syllabus — a framework for training young Kenyans to think clearly, argue ethically, and lead with purpose.

March 5, 2025 · 6 min read
Read More →

There is a version of debate that exists only as a game. Points are scored, winners declared, and the substance of what was argued evaporates the moment the session ends. This is not what TDC does.


The decision to anchor TDC's entire competitive programme in the 17 UN Sustainable Development Goals was deliberate and, we believe, transformative. Not because the SDGs are fashionable — though they are — but because they are genuinely the most rigorous publicly available framework for thinking about the world's most important problems. When students debate SDG 13 (Climate Action) in Nakuru, they are engaging with a real, urgent, global challenge through a local lens. When they debate SDG 1 (No Poverty) in Garissa, the motion is not abstract.


This is what makes TDC debates different from most competitive debate formats: the stakes are real.


The SDG framework also solves a structural problem with youth civic engagement — it gives young people a legitimate entry point. It says: here are 17 areas where the world has agreed action is needed. Pick one. Argue about it. What do you think? This is not condescending. It is the most serious invitation you can extend to a 16-year-old who cares about the world they're inheriting.


Across TDC's 2025 season, we saw the framework produce remarkable specificity. At the Coast, students debated SDG 14 (Life Below Water) in terms of fishing community livelihoods and tourism economics — issues their families navigate daily. In the North Rift, SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) was not a development-economics abstraction but a debate about the food systems that determine whether Kitale's agricultural communities thrive or fail. In Nairobi, KDIC-partnered motions on SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities) and SDG 8 (Decent Work) connected students to Kenya's financial institutions in ways that traditional financial education never has.


The result, year after year, is a cohort of young Kenyans who do not wait to be given permission to have opinions about the world. They have already argued those opinions in front of peers and adjudicators. They have been challenged, pushed back on, and asked to do better. They emerge from TDC's circuit not with answers — but with the confidence, rigour, and intellectual humility to keep asking the right questions.


That, more than any trophy, is the point.
